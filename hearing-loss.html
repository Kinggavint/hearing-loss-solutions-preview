What is hearing loss?

More than 48 million Americans — over 20% of the population — report difficulty hearing. If you do not hear as well as you once did, you are far from alone, and it is far more treatable than most people realize.

By the Numbers

Hearing loss is more common — and younger — than you think.

One of the most persistent myths is that only “older people” have hearing loss. In fact, the majority of people with hearing loss are younger than 65.

Common causes

Hearing loss has many roots, but exposure to noise ranks high among them. The primary causes we see are:

  • Exposure to loud or prolonged noise
  • Family history of hearing loss
  • Certain medications (ototoxicity)
  • The natural aging process
  • Disease and infection
  • Head trauma

Why it matters

Untreated hearing loss has been linked to emotional, physical, mental, and even economic disadvantages — from social isolation and fatigue to a well-documented association with cognitive decline.

The encouraging news: with the right evaluation and a plan built around how you actually live and listen, most hearing loss is highly manageable. The sooner it is addressed, the better the outcome.

The Four Types

Not all hearing loss is the same.

The type of hearing loss determines the treatment. A thorough diagnostic evaluation tells us exactly what we are working with — and what will actually help.

Conductive

Sound is blocked from reaching the inner ear — sometimes by something as simple and reversible as earwax buildup or fluid.

Sensorineural

The most common permanent type, caused when the tiny hair cells in the cochlea are missing or damaged. Highly treatable with the right technology.

Mixed

A combination of conductive and sensorineural hearing loss, calling for a plan that addresses both the blockage and the nerve component.

Central

Originates in the central nervous system — often associated with stroke or neurological disease — and is evaluated with specialized testing.
